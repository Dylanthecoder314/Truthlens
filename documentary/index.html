The Vanishing ofMH370

On 8 March 2014 a Malaysia Airlines Boeing 777 with 239 people on board went silent over the South China Sea, turned around and flew into the night. Twelve years and three seabed searches later, it has still not been found.

PrologueKuala Lumpur International Airport

239 on board

MH370 was the overnight service from Kuala Lumpur to Beijing, a flight of about five and a half hours. On board were 227 passengers, two of them infants, and 12 Malaysian crew.

Most passengers were Chinese citizens, among them a group of calligraphers and painters returning from an exhibition in Kuala Lumpur. Twenty were employees of the chip maker Freescale Semiconductor. Two were young Iranian men travelling on stolen European passports; police concluded they were migrants trying to reach Europe, with no link to what happened.

Chapter 0100:42 MYT · Sat 8 Mar 201416:42 UTC · 7 Mar

Departure

MH370 took off at 00:42, a few minutes behind schedule. The aircraft, registration 9M-MRO, was a Boeing 777-200ER that Malaysia Airlines had flown since 2002.

In the cockpit were Captain Zaharie Ahmad Shah, 53, who had joined the airline in 1981 and had more than 18,000 flying hours, and First Officer Fariq Abdul Hamid, 27, who had recently moved onto the 777.

The plan was routine. Climb northeast across the peninsula and out over the South China Sea, hand over to Vietnamese air traffic control at a waypoint called IGARI, and land in Beijing at 06:30. At 01:01 the aircraft levelled off at 35,000 feet. At 01:07 it sent an automatic ACARS maintenance report, as it did every half hour. It was the last one. The next report, due at 01:37, never arrived.

Chapter 0201:19:30 MYT17:19:30 UTC

“Good night”

As MH370 approached the edge of Malaysian airspace, the Kuala Lumpur controller told the crew to contact Ho Chi Minh City. The reply was the last thing anyone heard from the aircraft.

MH370 to Kuala Lumpur Radar, 01:19:30“Good night. Malaysian three seven zero.”

The crew never called Ho Chi Minh. At 01:21:13, as the aircraft passed IGARI, its transponder stopped transmitting. The transponder is what tells civilian radar which plane it is seeing and at what altitude. Without it, MH370 disappeared from controllers' screens.

Malaysian military radar works differently. It bounces radio waves off anything in the sky, so it kept recording an unidentified aircraft. Moments after IGARI, that aircraft turned back to the southwest. The 2018 investigation concluded the turn was most likely flown by hand, not by the autopilot.

At 01:38, controllers in Ho Chi Minh City called Kuala Lumpur to ask where MH370 was. Calls went back and forth between the two control centres, the airline and the military for hours. The Kuala Lumpur rescue coordination centre was not activated until 05:30.

Chapter 0301:21–02:22 MYT17:21–18:22 UTC

A ghost on military radar

The unidentified aircraft flew back across the Malay Peninsula, passed just south of Penang Island at about 01:52 and headed out over the Strait of Malacca. It passed the tiny island of Pulau Perak at about 02:03, then followed a line close to the airway that leads toward India and the Middle East.

Military radar recorded the aircraft but no one sent planes to intercept it. Its last radar return came at 02:22, about 200 nautical miles northwest of Penang. No radar ever saw MH370 again.

Search crews spent the first days in the South China Sea, along the planned route. On 15 March, Prime Minister Najib Razak said the aircraft's movements were consistent with deliberate action by someone on the plane.

Chapter 0402:25–08:19 MYT18:25–00:19 UTC

Seven handshakes

MH370 left one more trail. At 02:25 its satellite terminal, which had been off, logged back on to the Inmarsat-3F1 satellite parked 35,800 km above the Indian Ocean. For the next six hours, a ground station in Perth and the aircraft exchanged brief automatic signals called handshakes. They carried no position and no message.

Each handshake did record two numbers. The time a signal took to make the round trip gives the distance between the aircraft and the satellite, which places the plane somewhere on a ring around the point beneath the satellite. The shift in the signal's frequency reflects the plane's motion, and that pattern matched a track to the south, not the north.

Twice the airline phoned the aircraft over the satellite link, at 02:39 and 07:13. No one answered. At 06:30, when MH370 should have been landing in Beijing, it was still flying and still answering handshakes.

The last complete handshake came at 08:11. At 08:19:29 the aircraft itself asked to log on again: a partial handshake. Investigators believe this is what happens when both engines run out of fuel and a backup power unit starts, briefly reviving the satellite terminal. When the ground station called at 09:15, nothing answered.

That final ring became known as the 7th arc. The last satellite data are consistent with a steep, fast descent, which puts the wreck close to the arc. On 24 March, after analysis by Inmarsat and the UK Air Accidents Investigation Branch, Najib told the world what the data meant.

“It is therefore with deep sadness and regret that I must inform you that, according to this new data, flight MH370 ended in the southern Indian Ocean.”

Najib Razak, Prime Minister of Malaysia, 24 March 2014

Chapter 05March 2014 – May 2018Southern Indian Ocean

The search

The hunt for MH370 became the most expensive search in aviation history. In March and April 2014, ships and aircraft from many countries combed the ocean surface west of Perth. Satellites spotted hundreds of floating objects. None came from the plane.

In early April, the Australian ship Ocean Shield picked up acoustic pings that seemed to come from the aircraft's flight recorder beacons. A robot submarine searched the seabed beneath and found nothing. Investigators later concluded the signals had not come from MH370.

From October 2014, ships towing sonar swept the seafloor along the 7th arc, over undersea mountains that had never been mapped, in water up to six kilometres deep. The search was led by the Australian Transport Safety Bureau and funded by Malaysia, Australia and China. It covered more than 120,000 km² and cost about A$200 million. On 17 January 2017 the three governments suspended it.

“It is almost inconceivable and certainly societally unacceptable in the modern aviation era … for a large commercial aircraft to be missing and for the world not to know with certainty what became of the aircraft and those on board.”

Australian Transport Safety Bureau, final search report, 3 October 2017

In January 2018 the marine robotics company Ocean Infinity began a private search on a “no find, no fee” basis, using a fleet of autonomous underwater vehicles launched from the ship Seabed Constructor. In about four months it covered more than 112,000 km², mostly north of the earlier search area. It found nothing, and the search ended in late May 2018.

Chapter 06July 2015 onwardWestern Indian Ocean coastlines

What the sea gave back

On 29 July 2015, workers cleaning a beach at Saint-André on Réunion Island found a two-metre section of aircraft wing covered in barnacles. French investigators confirmed it was a flaperon from 9M-MRO. It was the first physical proof that MH370 had ended in the ocean.

More pieces followed, carried thousands of kilometres west by ocean currents to the coasts of East Africa and the islands of the western Indian Ocean. Many were found by local beachgoers and by the American amateur investigator Blaine Gibson, who spent years walking remote beaches. Of 27 items examined for the 2018 safety report, three were confirmed as coming from MH370 and 16 more were judged almost certain or highly likely.

The debris narrowed the problem in two ways. Drift modelling by Australia's science agency CSIRO, run backwards from where and when pieces washed ashore, pointed to a crash site on the 7th arc, most likely north of the area searched by 2017. And the flaperon and flap sections suggested the wing flaps were retracted when the aircraft hit the water. That argues against a controlled ditching, where a pilot would normally extend the flaps to land slowly on the sea.

Chapter 0730 July 2018Safety Investigation Report

The official answer

Malaysia's international safety investigation team published its final report in July 2018, a document of roughly 1,500 pages including appendices. It could not determine why MH370 disappeared. Its main findings:

  • The turn back after IGARI and later course changes were most likely made under manual control, not by the autopilot.
  • Investigators found no evidence of abnormal behaviour, stress or financial trouble in the backgrounds of the captain or first officer.
  • No mechanical fault was identified that would explain the flight path. The team said it could not rule out unlawful interference by a third party.
  • Air traffic control failed to follow procedures. Kuala Lumpur was slow to start emergency procedures, did not monitor radar continuously, relied on the airline's information and did not call on the military for help.

The report did not name anyone as responsible. Its authors were clear that a definitive answer would require finding the wreckage and the flight recorders.

Chapter 08Fact check

Claims and evidence

Few events have produced more theories. Here is how the best-known ones hold up against the evidence that exists.

  1. ✕Contradicted

    It crashed in the South China Sea, or was shot down near its planned route.

    Military radar tracked the aircraft flying west for an hour after IGARI, satellite handshakes continued for six more hours, and confirmed wreckage washed up thousands of kilometres away in the western Indian Ocean.

  2. ✕Contradicted

    It landed intact somewhere, such as Central Asia or a remote island.

    The satellite frequency data fit a southern route, not a northern one. The final signals point to a high-speed descent, and parts of the aircraft have come ashore in Africa and on Indian Ocean islands.

  3. ?Unproven

    One of the pilots deliberately diverted the aircraft.

    The manual turns and a route that slipped between radar coverage suggest someone who knew the aircraft well. Data recovered from the captain's home flight simulator included a simulated flight deep into the southern Indian Ocean, flown weeks earlier. Malaysian police said they found nothing else unusual, and the official investigation found no evidence in either pilot's background and named no one.

  4. ?Unproven

    Hijackers took control of the aircraft.

    No group claimed responsibility and there was no ransom demand. Background checks on passengers found no links to terrorism. The 2018 report said third-party interference could not be ruled out, but it found no evidence for it.

  5. ↓Unlikely

    A fire or mechanical failure disabled the crew, and the plane flew on as a “ghost flight”.

    A disabled crew could explain hours of straight flight south on autopilot. It explains much less well the sequence of turns after IGARI, which the investigation found were most likely flown by hand, or the path that tracked a published airway.

  6. ?Unproven

    Amateur radio data (WSPR) has pinpointed the crash site.

    Engineer Richard Godfrey argues that disturbances in weak radio signals sent by amateur stations traced the aircraft's flight. Many radio experts doubt such signals can detect an aircraft at all, and the method has not been independently validated.

  7. …Open question

    Underwater microphones could show where it hit the sea.

    A 2024 Cardiff University study found that hydrophones run by the nuclear test monitoring network should be able to detect a large aircraft striking the ocean, and urged a fresh look at the recordings from that morning. No signal has yet been tied to MH370.

Chapter 092025–2027Ocean Infinity returns

Still searching

In December 2024 Malaysia agreed in principle to let Ocean Infinity search again on the same terms: no find, no fee, with US$70 million payable only if the wreck is found. The company's ship Armada 86 05 began work in late February 2025, and the contract was signed on 25 March. Bad weather in the southern Indian Ocean halted the search in April.

The ship returned on 30 December 2025 and searched until 23 January 2026. Across both phases it logged 28 search days and surveyed about 7,571 km², roughly half of a 15,000 km² target area chosen from the latest analysis. On 8 March 2026, the twelfth anniversary, Malaysia's transport ministry announced that nothing had been found.

In June 2026 the cabinet extended the contract by a year, to 30 June 2027, so the company can survey the remaining 7,428.54 km². The terms did not change. As in previous seasons, the work depends on the calmer weather of the southern summer.

For the families, the search is only part of it. Next-of-kin groups such as Voice370 have spent twelve years pressing governments to keep looking and to share what they know. In December 2025 a Beijing court ordered Malaysia Airlines to pay about 2.9 million yuan to each of eight families. Another 23 cases were still pending, and 47 families had settled with the airline.
